# S4. Hardware and edge-transport rationale

## 4.2 Capture Layer: Pose-Constrained AR Acquisition

Choice of headset category and device. The decision to build the capture layer around a head-mounted display, rather than a handheld device or a fixed industrial monitor, is grounded in the adoption literature rather than a purely internal preference. In their PRISMA-based systematic review of AR adoption strategies in maintenance, covering 80 peer-reviewed and gray-literature sources published between 2017 and 2025, Tayyarat et al. identify critical success factors for AR adoption in maintenance workflows (Tayyarat et al., 2025b). Among the AR tools reported in maintenance contexts (n = 11), head-mounted displays were the most frequent category (45.4%), ahead of mobile devices (27.3%), industrial monitors (18.2%), and projection systems (9.1%) (Tayyarat et al., 2025b). This predominance of head-mounted displays (HMDs) in maintenance-type tasks supports the choice of a headset over a tablet-based or fixed-screen solution for a hands-on inspection task where the technician must manipulate the pantograph and read measurements simultaneously.

Within the HMD category, Meta Quest 3 offers several advantages that make it a suitable platform for the proposed mixed-reality inspection system. Its full-color passthrough, provided by the dual RGB front-facing cameras, is essential for superimposing the segmentation overlay directly onto the technician’s real-world view of the pantograph rather than relying on a reconstructed scene. The standalone XR2 Gen 2 platform supports the on-headset functions required by the proposed architecture, including passthrough rendering, environment capture, pose tracking, user interaction, response decoding, and AR-overlay presentation, without requiring a tethered workstation. Compute-intensive model inference remains offloaded to the local edge server described in Section 4.3. In addition, the headset provides native 6-DOF spatial tracking through SLAM, as well as hand tracking, allowing the system to maintain stable spatial registration between the virtual overlay and the physical pantograph. Its field of view of approximately 104° × 96° is sufficiently wide to keep both the guide reticle and the pantograph within the technician’s field of view simultaneously (Meta Platforms, 2026a). Finally, Meta provides an accessible developer stack, including the Passthrough Camera API and MRUK, which exposes the main primitives required by the proposed pipeline: camera frames for the segmentation stream and world-locked spatial anchors for maintaining the position of the overlay, as described in Section 4.5.

However, Meta Quest 3 also presents several limitations. Its battery life is approximately 2.2 hours on average (Meta Platforms, 2026a), meaning that spare batteries or scheduled battery changes would be required to support a complete inspection shift. The absence of a native MDM or kiosk mode also complicates device management if the system is deployed across multiple technicians or workshops. The main limitation is that the headset’s native depth estimates were not validated as a metrological reference for this application. Meta’s Depth API exposes real-time, per-eye, per-frame environment depth estimates from the headset’s point of view, and its reference samples use them for dynamic occlusion (Meta Platforms, 2026d). Because the facility’s disposition thresholds are expressed in millimeters and the metric accuracy of the headset depth stream was not characterized in this study, headset depth was not used as the measurement reference. Consequently, while Quest 3 is well suited for visual guidance, segmentation visualization, spatial registration, and operator interaction, its depth-sensing capabilities were not relied upon for dimensional measurements of pantograph components.

Why not use a more precise headset? Some enterprise headsets provide native depth sensors with published accuracy evaluations. HoloLens 2’s time-of-flight sensor, for instance, has been independently evaluated using both AHAT and Long-Throw modes, with reported mean depth-overestimation errors of 5.7 mm and 9.0 mm, respectively (Kerkhof et al., 2025). This capability, however, does not change the choice, for two reasons. First, the thickness measurement pipeline does not use any headset's built-in depth sensor (see below), so a more accurate depth sensor would not give a more accurate thickness reading here. Second, enterprise-oriented headsets such as HoloLens 2 or Magic Leap 2 occupy a distinctly higher cost tier than consumer-grade standalone devices (launch list prices of approximately USD 3,500 for HoloLens 2 and USD 3,299 for Magic Leap 2, versus USD 499 for the 128 GB Quest 3), a difference that matters far more once the deployment target is an entire technician population across multiple workshops rather than a single demonstrator unit (VRcompare, 2026). The relevant trade-off is therefore cost per deployed unit, not raw sensor precision: paying for depth accuracy that the architecture does not use offers no return, whereas the Quest 3 is paired with the geometry-based capture protocol described below, which does not depend on headset depth, at a much lower cost at scale. On this cost basis the Quest 3 was the more defensible choice, even though it is not the most precise device available.

Continuous capture stream. The headset performs continuous environment capture and submits frames to the edge server through a sequential HTTP request-response loop. The client is configured with a minimum request interval of 0.1 s, corresponding to a maximum nominal request-attempt frequency of 10 Hz. Because only one request may remain active at a time, a new frame is submitted only after the preceding request has been completed. The effective analysis frequency is therefore determined by the complete capture, communication, inference, response-decoding, and rendering cycle rather than by the nominal interval alone. Each submitted frame carries the pose and image data required by the coarse-to-fine segmentation pipeline described in Section 4.4.

Pose-constrained thickness acquisition. Thickness estimation is not triggered from an arbitrary frame of the continuous stream because the method requires a near-profile view of the complete carbon strip. As illustrated in Fig. 7, the application displays an AR alignment frame that guides the technician toward the required capture viewpoint. Capture is validated only when the segmented carbon-layer overlay is fully visible within the frame and aligned with the AR guide within an angular tolerance of ±5°. The ±5° acceptance tolerance was introduced to limit perspective-induced geometric error during monocular measurement. Under a simplified cosine projection model, a 5° angular deviation corresponds to a projected-length deviation of approximately 0.4% . This tolerance was therefore used as an acquisition-control criterion rather than as a metrological uncertainty bound. Until both conditions are satisfied, the technician receives visual repositioning feedback. This procedure limits yaw-induced foreshortening and reduces viewpoint-related variability before dimensional estimation.

Although the segmentation class is named contact_strip, its mask delineates only the visible carbon layer, excluding the metallic carrier and mounting structure. Accordingly, represents the remaining carbon-layer thickness in pixels.

From pixels to millimeters. Thickness images were acquired at a resolution of 1280 × 960 pixels. The captured frames were subsequently resized to the 1920-pixel model input resolution before inference. This resizing standardizes the model input dimensions but does not increase the native spatial information available for metric estimation. For each validated capture, the image-specific scale factor was recalculated from its corresponding segmented carbon-layer length. Remaining carbon thickness was evaluated at three sections along the segmented mask. The minimum of the three estimated values was retained as the representative thickness, following the same worst-case principle as the manual procedure. This minimum was compared with the 14 mm acceptance threshold; a value below the threshold flagged the strip for replacement.

## 4.3 Edge-Server Inference Layer

The choice to offload inference to an edge server, rather than running the four coupled models (YOLO, Mask R-CNN, U-Net, DeepLabV3+) directly on the headset, is driven mainly by on-device compute capacity. The Meta Quest 3's standalone XR2 Gen 2 processor must already sustain passthrough rendering, tracking, and overlay presentation; adding the stacked, coarse-to-fine four-model pipeline on-device was expected to compete with these functions and increase the risk of overlay lag relative to head movement. On-device inference throughput was not benchmarked in this study. Full cloud inference is ruled out next: routing every captured frame to a remote datacenter adds a round trip over the public internet whose latency is both higher and less predictable than a local link. This is the class of problem that edge computing was defined to address, by moving computation from the cloud to the network edge to meet response-time requirements that centralized cloud processing cannot guarantee (W. Shi et al., 2016). Offloading compute-intensive inference from a wearable AR device to a nearby server, rather than to either extreme, is an established architectural pattern: Ha et al. demonstrated it for a Google-Glass-based cognitive-assistance system (Ha et al., 2014), and a later empirical study of the same class of application showed edge computing to be necessary, not just beneficial, to meet the latency targets these systems require (Chen et al., 2017). In the present implementation, the on-premises edge server handled one headset inference stream at a time over the local network, avoiding dependence on a remote cloud path.

Implementation. The edge server exposes the segmentation pipeline as a stateless HTTP inference endpoint (FastAPI), implemented in the edge-inference-server module of the project repository ([anonymized repository link] rather than a persistent low-level streaming protocol. The Unity/Meta XR SDK application running on the headset submits one captured frame per HTTP request; the server runs it through the full coarse-to-fine pipeline (Section 4.4) and returns a single JSON response bundling, in one round trip, the per-strip defect metrics (crack and spalling percentages, orientation, alert flags) and a transparent PNG overlay, encoded in base64 so it can be decoded directly on-device and composited over the live passthrough feed, rather than requiring the client to parse a separate binary stream. The edge server was equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5070 Laptop GPU with 8 GB of dedicated video memory. This hardware configuration was used for the latency measurements reported in Section 6.3 and Tables 11 and 12. The inference environment relied on NVIDIA’s CUDA/TensorRT software stack for Blackwell-generation GPUs (NVIDIA Corporation, 2025). The client uses a nominal analysis-request interval of 0.1 s. Since requests are processed sequentially and only one request may remain active at a time, this setting represents a maximum request-attempt frequency of 10 Hz rather than a guaranteed end-to-end inference rate. Why HTTP request–response rather than WebRTC. Real-time XR/AR streaming research generally identifies WebRTC-class protocols (peer-to-peer transport, dedicated data channels, sub-frame latency) as the predominant choice for continuous, high-frequency camera or rendered-content streaming under strict synchronization constraints (Mejías et al., 2025). WebRTC's benefit, however, is concentrated in the network-transport segment of the latency budget (connection reuse, UDP transport, no per-frame handshake) and requires substantially more engineering: a signaling server, ICE/STUN/TURN negotiation, and third-party WebRTC stacks on both the Unity/Quest 3 client and the Python edge server, none of which currently ship as a native Unity/Quest package. The HTTP request-response implementation was therefore retained for the present field evaluation because it provided sufficient responsiveness with lower implementation complexity than a dedicated media-streaming stack.
