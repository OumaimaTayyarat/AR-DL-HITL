# S3. Dataset and architecture-screening details

## 4.4.1 Contact-Strip Localization

Localizing the contact strip is the first step in the segmentation process and directly affects the reliability of subsequent defect detection. It restricts the analysis to the specific surface targeted by the visual inspection, which is essential given the complex geometry of the train roof. By excluding other surfaces and structural elements, this preliminary localization focuses the analysis on the relevant area and reduces false detections.

Review of existing datasets. Prior to constructing a training dataset, a PRISMA-informed review of existing pantograph-related datasets was conducted (Fig. 8; full screening pipeline). All PRISMA-informed searches reported in Section 4.4 were conducted on 24 July 2026 in IEEE Xplore, ScienceDirect, Springer, and ACM, covering 2017–2025. For pantograph-related datasets, 25 sources were identified, of which 12 met the selection criteria. These findings motivated the construction of a dedicated dataset using mixed acquisition. From a 9-minute handheld field video containing 12,930 frames, 500 representative frames were selected to reduce temporal redundancy. These frames were complemented by 50 manually acquired field photographs covering additional viewpoints, wear conditions, defects, and lighting scenarios. The resulting 550 unique source images were annotated in COCO JSON polygon format under a single contact_strip class representing the visible carbon layer. Following data augmentation, the final dataset comprised 2,178 images derived from 540 of the 550 source images (500 video frames and 50 photographs; 10 frames fell within exclusion buffers). The video frames were partitioned at the level of source-frame blocks, and the photographs were distributed across subsets as independent units. The resulting training, validation, and test subsets comprised 1,565, 286, and 327 images, respectively. Table 5 and Fig. 9 summarize the dataset and acquisition workflow.

Architecture screening. A parallel PRISMA-informed search of the same four databases (2017–2025, keywords: "pantograph" AND "deep learning" AND ("segmentation" OR "detection")) retrieved 155 records, of which 30 were retained after selection criteria (English, peer-reviewed, railway/pantograph focus, deep-learning-based, reporting quantitative metrics). Twenty-two unique architectures were extracted and consolidated to 12 candidates, each scored on a five-criterion decision matrix (literature prevalence, pantograph-specific relevance, reported accuracy, inference speed, code availability) (Fig. 10). Three candidates were shortlisted for direct experimental validation: Mask R-CNN (ResNet-50-FPN), PIDNet-MSP, and DeepLabV3+. The complete 12-architecture matrix, individual criterion scores, supporting references, and screening decisions are provided as a supplementary table in the public repository: [anonymized repository link]

Fig. 10. PRISMA-informed model-selection and experimental-validation workflow for contact-strip localization, from literature screening through decision-matrix scoring to final model selection. Source: authors.

Experimental comparison. All three shortlisted candidates were trained and evaluated using the dataset described above. For the localization dataset, splitting was group-aware rather than random, to prevent leakage between augmented variants and temporally adjacent source frames. Source frames were organized into contiguous temporal blocks of 10 frames, and each block, together with all augmented variants derived from its frames, was assigned exclusively to the training, validation, or test subset. A five-frame exclusion buffer was applied at each boundary between subsets (10 frames in total) to further reduce temporal leakage.

Model fitting was performed on the training subset, while checkpoint selection and early stopping were based exclusively on the validation subset. The fixed test subset comprised 327 images, derived from 82 original images (80 video frames in 8 blocks and 2 photographs) and their augmented variants. It was used to compare the three shortlisted localization architectures and to inform the final architecture choice. It was not used for gradient updates, training hyperparameter tuning, or checkpoint selection. Because consecutive frames and their augmented variants are strongly correlated, 95% confidence intervals were estimated with a block bootstrap over the 8 test blocks (10,000 resamples), recomputing the pooled IoU and Dice for each resample. Consequently, the reported results provide an internal architecture comparison rather than an independent post-selection estimate of the selected model’s generalization performance. Augmented variants were assigned to the same subset as their source block; no variant of a training or validation frame entered the test subset. The training protocol used cosine-annealing learning-rate scheduling, validation-loss-based checkpointing, early stopping with a patience of eight epochs, and gradient-norm clipping. PIDNet-MSP achieved a lower loss than Mask R-CNN, but loss values are not directly sufficient to establish superior generalization or overfitting, particularly across architectures with different objectives and output formulations. The architecture decision was therefore based on the complete metric profile, with Mask R-CNN achieving the highest IoU and Dice scores on the common evaluation subset.

YOLO-based region proposal and detection trade-off. YOLOv8n was not included in the segmentation-architecture shortlist because it served a different function: coarse region proposal rather than pixel-level carbon-layer delineation. Applied directly to the full inspection frame, Mask R-CNN remained susceptible to false-positive masks on structures resembling contact strips elsewhere on the pantograph assembly and surrounding roof equipment. A YOLOv8n detector was therefore placed upstream of the segmentation stage to propose candidate contact-strip regions. The model was initialized with COCO-pretrained weights and fine-tuned for 150 epochs using a 640 × 640 input resolution and a batch size of 8. The confidence threshold was fixed at 0.4 on the validation subset. Each accepted bounding box was expanded by a 15% margin and passed to Mask R-CNN for carbon-layer segmentation (Fig. 11).

On the held-out test subset (327 images containing approximately 990 annotated contact-strip instances), the two-stage YOLO–Mask R-CNN pipeline produced 20 false-positive strip detections, compared with 40 for full-frame Mask R-CNN. This reduction was accompanied by 50 additional missed ground-truth strip instances relative to full-frame Mask R-CNN (approximately 5% of the annotated instances), because no segmentation was performed when YOLO failed to generate a valid proposal. On the same test subset, YOLOv8n achieved a precision of 0.940, a recall of 0.930, and an mAP@0.5 of 0.960. These results indicate a detection trade-off rather than an unconditional improvement. Evaluated as deployed, the two-stage pipeline reached an IoU of 0.873, a precision of 0.975, and a recall of 0.893, compared with 0.905, 0.960, and 0.940 for full-frame Mask R-CNN. In this safety-critical setting, a missed strip is the more consequential error, because no segmentation, overlay, or defect assessment is produced for it. The proposal stage was nevertheless retained because it halved false-positive strip detections, which would otherwise produce spurious overlays and defect assessments on non-strip surfaces. End-to-end missed-defect results are reported in Section 6.3.

The comparison in Table 6 already answers, at the localization stage alone, a question that motivates the rest of this section: whether architecture choice materially matters or whether any reasonably competent model would do. Evaluated on the same 327-image test subset under a common evaluation protocol, the three candidates achieved contact-strip IoU values ranging from 0.507 to 0.905. DeepLabV3+ obtained a Boundary IoU of 0.450 and a recall of 0.620, indicating weaker boundary agreement and lower sensitivity for contact-strip segmentation than the other evaluated configurations. This is a single, relatively homogeneous localization task, before any defect-type distinction is introduced. This internal result is the first argument against collapsing the pipeline into a single universal model: if architecture choice already produces this spread on one target class, there is no basis to expect a single model to hold up uniformly once the task is split into structurally dissimilar defect classes downstream.

This expectation is confirmed directly in the literature on the same defect classes targeted by this study. A comparison between the Segment Anything Model (SAM), used zero-shot as a universal segmentation backbone, and U-Net on civil-infrastructure cracking and spalling found that SAM outperforms on elongated, longitudinal cracks while U-Net outperforms on compact spalling regions; no single model won across both classes (Ahmadi et al., 2025). This divergence has a measured geometric basis rather than being an architectural artifact: cracks have a mean aspect ratio around 4.4, while spalling and net-crack regions have a compactness close to 1.00 (Arafin et al., 2024). Attempts to close this gap with a single foundation model rather than defect-specific architectures required substantial hybridization to reach competitive performance: a SAM model fine-tuned with a dedicated loss function reached only mIoU 0.59–0.69 on crack data, requiring integration with a secondary Detectron2 stage to reach mIoU ≈0.83 (Rakshitha et al., 2024). Task-appropriate point-cloud segmentation of concrete surfaces similarly required defect-aware, severity-differentiated modeling rather than a single generic classifier to separate crack, spall, and severe-spall categories reliably (Bolourian et al., 2023). Even within a single architecture family, elongated-crack morphology has been shown to require targeted structural adaptation (multi-scale feature fusion, attention) before an otherwise generic U-Net performs competitively on cracks (Wu et al., 2023). This is the same pattern already observed internally between DeepLabV3+ and Mask R-CNN in Table 6.

The strongest evidence, however, concerns the cost of getting the class wrong rather than raw segmentation accuracy. A multi-class concrete-defect classifier, built specifically for the task rather than shared naively across classes, has been shown to exhibit a measurable, non-zero rate of confusion between adjacent defect types: 4.69% between scaling and severe crack, and 6.25% between severe crack and spalling (Mayya and Alkayem, 2025). In a generic reporting context, this would be an acceptable trade-off for architectural simplicity. It is not acceptable here, because defect type in this pipeline is not a descriptive label but a routing key: it determines which dimensional tolerance is applied to a given measurement and which follow-up question the human-in-the-loop correction workflow asks the technician (Section 4.6). A crack misclassified as spalling, or the reverse, does not simply mislabel an image. It evaluates a real defect against the wrong tolerance criterion, either clearing a unit that should have been flagged or triggering a replacement decision on a criterion that does not apply. A confusion rate of a few percent, harmless in a generic benchmark, is the failure mode this deployment cannot tolerate.

Together, the internal comparison in Table 6, the class-dependent performance reversal reported for the same defect types in the literature, and the quantified operational cost of cross-class confusion converge on the same conclusion: a shared, single architecture across defect types cannot be assumed to hold, and the choice between defect-specific branches must be settled experimentally rather than by architectural convenience. This motivates the dedicated architecture screening and comparative evaluation conducted separately for cracking and spalling below.

## 4.4.2 Crack and Spalling Dataset and Architecture Selection

Defect datasets: review and construction. A dedicated PRISMA-informed review targeting the keyword combination "crack dataset" AND "spalling dataset" AND "segmentation" across the same four databases (search date and period as above) retrieved 307 raw records; after deduplication (180 excluded) and title/abstract screening (127 reviewed, 65 excluded as topically irrelevant), 62 records were assessed on introduction/conclusion, yielding 22 studies meeting at least one inclusion criterion: A) creation of a pixel-level annotated crack or spalling dataset, B) dataset augmentation (GAN, diffusion, semi-supervised, pseudo-labeling), or C) benchmark use of a public dataset with quantitative metrics (Fig. 12). Across these 22 sources, only 9 are fully open-source, the remainder mostly restricted or single-domain (road, concrete, tunnel, bridge), and none targets pantograph carbon contact strips. This confirms, at the defect level, the same absence already established for pantograph-specific data at the localization stage: crack and spalling inspection is not a routine practice in high-speed rail maintenance, real defect instances remain scarce even with facility access, and the contact strip presents an atypical target surface for direct transfer: dark, homogeneous, low-contrast, and narrow in geometry, such that crack segmentation applied to full pantograph imagery is dominated by background false positives (metal fixings, cabling, structural elements).

To bridge this gap without discarding the scale of existing generic crack corpora, a three-phase progressive curriculum was adopted (Fig. 13): a general-domain phase comprising 11,298 pixel-annotated images aggregated from CFD (Y. Shi et al., 2016), CrackSeg9k (Kulkarni et al., 2022), DeepCrack (Liu et al., 2019; Zou et al., 2019), Crack500 (Yang et al., 2020), and GAPs384 (Eisenbach et al., 2017), after removal of images duplicated across these sources; a domain-approximation phase comprising 167 ceramic surface-crack images (Junior et al., 2021); and a target-domain phase comprising 150 pantograph contact-strip images with pixel-level crack annotations. This sequential fine-tuning, encoder frozen between stages, was preferred over merging all sources into a single corpus, since pantograph images would represent only about 1.3% of such a merged set (a share too small to shape training under uniform sampling), while the three domains remain visually heterogeneous enough that naive mixing would require complex, less-controllable rebalancing to prevent the target signal from being drowned out.

Fig. 13. Three-phase progressive domain-bridging curriculum for crack-segmentation training, from generic crack corpora through a ceramic visual-proxy domain to the target pantograph contact-strip domain. Source: authors.

The same three-phase progressive curriculum was independently applied to spalling, using 9850 pixel-annotated images aggregated from dacl10k (Flotzinger et al., 2024), the RC structure segmentation dataset (Wang and Ueda, 2025), and MDMCS (Guo et al., 2026), from which only the spalling class was retained, 364 ceramic-tile spalling images for domain approximation (Wang and Wu, 2025), and 230 pixel-annotated pantograph contact-strip images for target-domain adaptation. The crack set (150 images) and the spalling set (230 images) consisted of independently acquired photographs. Unlike the video-derived localization data, they were split so that no physical strip appears in more than one subset, into 105 training, 22 validation, and 23 test images for cracking, and 161, 34, and 35 images for spalling. The general-domain and ceramic phases were used for pre-training only; all reported test metrics refer to the pantograph target-domain test sets.

Architecture screening for defect segmentation. A parallel PRISMA-informed search targeting defect-segmentation architectures applicable to both cracking and spalling (2017–2025, keywords: ("crack" OR "spalling") AND "segmentation" AND "deep learning") retrieved 200 records (IEEE Xplore: 70, ScienceDirect: 55, Springer: 40, ACM: 35). After removing 57 duplicates/out-of-period records and 113 further exclusions against title/abstract criteria (English, peer-reviewed, segmentation task, architecture explicitly proposed, quantitative metrics reported), 30 articles were retained. Twenty-two unique architectures were extracted and consolidated to 18 candidates after removing four lacking pixel-level mask output. Each was scored on the same five-criterion, 25-point decision matrix used at the localization stage. Three architecture families were retained for experimental validation: DeepLabV3+, U-Net-based architectures, and a CNN-Transformer hybrid. Their concrete implementations were selected separately for each defect branch to reflect the differing morphology of cracking and spalling. The cracking comparison included DeepLabV3+, Hybrid U-Net++, and a CNN-Transformer hybrid, whereas the spalling comparison included U-Net, U-Net++, and DeepLabV3+. The encoder was fixed for each candidate before the test-subset evaluation. For cracking, the encoder of DeepLabV3+ was selected on the validation subset between ResNet34 and EfficientNet-B5, and EfficientNet-B5 gave the lowest validation loss. The CNN-Transformer hybrid used ResNet34 and Hybrid U-Net++ used ResNet34. For spalling, all three candidates used ResNet34. U-Net was not evaluated on cracking, and the CNN-Transformer hybrid was not evaluated on spalling.
